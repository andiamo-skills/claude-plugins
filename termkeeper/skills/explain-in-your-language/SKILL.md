# Explain in your language, keep the terms in the material's language

For people who study or work in another language (most often English) but understand explanations better in their native language. They still need to know the concepts **with the terms of the material**, because exams, meetings and documents use them.

## Which language the terms stay in
- The key terms stay in the **material's language**: the language of the slides, PDF or course. Everything below calls it the **term language**.
- Most of the time it is English. If the course is in Spanish, French, German or another language, keep the terms in that language instead (e.g. **flujo de caja libre**, **coste de capital**).
- If the material mixes languages, keep each term as the material writes it.

## Which language to explain in
- Use the language the user writes in. If they write in English but asked for another language before (or say so in their instructions), use that one.
- Everything below calls it the **explanation language**.

## When it applies
Whenever you explain, summarize or clarify material in the term language the user shared (slides, PDFs, readings, cases, exercises), or a concept from a subject taught in that language. It also applies when the explanation comes from another study skill or agent.

## Rules
- **Explanation in the explanation language**, clear and simple.
- **Always in the term language:** technical terms (WACC, EBITDA, free cash flow, beta), names of models and methods (DCF, CAPM, LBO), the course's main concepts, acronyms and formulas, and the keywords the user would need in an exam.
- **Bold** the term-language terms the first time they appear.
- **First time = short translation** in brackets, only if the meaning is not obvious. After that, only the original term.
- **Don't translate** terms the field already uses in the term language: no literal translation of "weighted average cost of capital" when everyone says **WACC**.
- **Quotes from the material:** if you report a definition or sentence from the slides, keep it in the term language, in quotation marks.
- **Formulas:** with the symbols and names used in the material.
- Everyday parts (verbs, connectors, examples) stay in the explanation language: don't mix languages at random.

## Glossary at the end
At the end of every **full** explanation (a lesson, a chapter, a whole topic) with at least 3 technical terms, add a short table:

| Term | Meaning in brief |
|---|---|
| **cost of equity** | return required by shareholders |

Only the terms that really matter for the exam or the job, 10 at most.

## Short questions get short answers
When the user asks something specific ("what is X?", "I don't get this step"), answer in **2-4 lines**: no glossary, no recap of the lesson, no follow-up questions. If they want more, they will ask.

## Quizzes and flashcards
If questions or flashcards come out of the explanation, the question can be in the explanation language, but the expected answer uses the term-language terms (e.g. "What rate is used to discount cash flows in a DCF?" → **WACC**).

## Example (explanation language: Italian, term language: English)
> Il **WACC** è il costo medio del capitale dell'azienda: pesa il **cost of equity** e il **cost of debt** **after-tax** (al netto delle tasse) in base alla **capital structure**. Nel **DCF** si usa come **discount rate** per scontare i **free cash flows**.
